555 and then Toulouse

For five and a half years nobody beat Jahangir Khan. Not once. Then a New Zealander who had lost to him 30 times walked on court in France.

11 November 1986 ·

555consecutive matches won
17Jahangir's age at his first world title
10British Open titles in a row, 1982 to 1991
9-1the fourth game that ended it

Jahangir Khan won the World Open in 1981 at 17, the youngest champion there had ever been. Then he simply did not lose. For the next five and a half years, across 555 matches, no one in the world could beat him. Guinness lists it as the longest winning run by any athlete in top-level professional sport.

Squash is brutal. There is nowhere to hide on a court, no teammates, no tactical draw. Jahangir trained to outlast everyone, and he did, match after match, tournament after tournament, year after year.

Ross Norman, from Whitianga in New Zealand, was the world number two. He had lost to Jahangir again and again, including in the 1985 World Open final. He kept telling people he would beat him one day.

The 1986 World Open final was in Toulouse on 11 November. Norman won the first game 9-5 and the second 9-7. Jahangir, as everyone expected, came back and took the third 9-7. Norman has said that from seven-all in that game he knew he had been two points from history.

In the fourth game the champion faded. Norman won it 9-1. The run was over.

It was Norman's first world title, and his only one. It was also the only match anyone needed to win to become a footnote in one of the greatest stories in sport.

What happened next

Jahangir won the World Open again in 1988 and retired in 1993. Norman, asked about it years later, said Jahangir had moved the game forward by at least 20 per cent.
